# POURS: access, experience and the brand promise

This is Double FW’s proposed framework for connecting accessibility, the quality of an experience and the promise an organisation makes to its customers. It preserves the emotional language of the original essay and adds a research and governance structure. It is guidance for review, not an adopted organisational policy, measured result or certification scheme.

## The relationship between POUR and POURS

WCAG has four principles: Perceivable, Operable, Understandable and Robust. They organise accessibility requirements; they do not establish that a whole customer experience is satisfactory or that a brand has delivered its promise. Accessibility and usability overlap. Describing WCAG as concerned only with technical functionality would obscure its attention to understanding, predictability, navigation and error handling.

POURS retains those four foundations and proposes **Satisfactory** as an additional Double FW experience principle. It does not amend WCAG. Satisfactory asks whether the experience meets the person’s needs and reasonable expectations while preserving dignity, identity and control. Expectations are evidence to investigate, not a ceiling: a customer accustomed to exclusion may report satisfaction despite a serious barrier.

Read three lenses together:

| Lens | Question | Appropriate evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Functional access | Can the person perceive, operate and understand the service with their chosen technology? | Applicable requirements, inspection, technical tests and observed tasks |
| Experience quality | Can they achieve the intended outcome with acceptable effort, time, choice and recovery? | Task outcomes, context, chosen or forced assistance, and the person’s account |
| Emotional and brand relationship | Does the encounter convey the organisation’s promise in a way this person recognises and values? | Voluntary participant accounts, observed contradictions and research over time |

These are related, but none proves the others. A participant can feel reassured while a security flaw remains. A technically accessible task can still be exhausting. A failed journey can still contain a respectful support interaction. Keep each finding visible.

## The five emotional descriptions

The following passages are retained verbatim from the original essay. Their functional lines are illustrations, not complete definitions or conformance tests. Their emotional language expresses intended qualities, not feelings that a designer can assign to every user. In particular, “all assistive technologies” expresses an aspiration; evidence must identify the combinations actually tested and the remaining gaps.

**Feeling Seen (was Perceivable)**
- Functional: Content exists in alternative formats.
- Emotional: No one scans the room and realises they're missing half the conversation. Users experience belonging, the world feels open to them, not gated behind a format they can't access.

**Feeling Capable (was Operable)**
- Functional: All functionality is available via keyboard.
- Emotional: Users experience agency. They don't hit invisible walls or feel like the system is working against them. Every action they intend produces a result. The interface whispers: you can do this.

**Feeling Confident (was Understandable)**
- Functional: Content is readable and predictable.
- Emotional: Users experience clarity. They know where they are, what happened, and what to do next. Navigation doesn't provoke doubt. Errors don't trigger shame. The world makes sense, even when it doesn't.

**Feeling Safe (was Robust)**
- Functional: Content works across all assistive technologies.
- Emotional: Users experience trust. They know the system won't betray them when their tool of choice changes, when their body changes, or when their circumstances shift. The experience holds. I can rely on this.

**Feeling Valued (was Satisfactory)**
- Functional: The experience meets needs without diminishing dignity or identity.
- Emotional: Users feel respected, included, and intentionally considered, not accommodated as an afterthought. The experience says: you belong here, and this was made with people like you in mind.

## Why this framing is useful

- Strong brand and inclusion language
- Connects directly to customer satisfaction and loyalty
- Moves beyond usability into emotional equity
- Aligns well with inclusive service design

These are reasons to investigate the framework. They are not evidence that using it causes loyalty, revenue or any particular emotion. Brand meaning varies between people, cultures, circumstances and moments in a journey. The intended relationship between each POUR domain and its emotional lens is a starting point, not a one-to-one psychological law. For example, inaccessible support can undermine all five feelings.

## Satisfactory as a fifth proposed principle

Satisfactory is broader than friendliness, delight or a favourable rating. It concerns navigation, correct completion, time and effort, choice and preferences, content consumption, voluntary engagement, continuity, recovery and dignity. It draws on Cognitive UX to reduce unnecessary demands without removing meaningful options or assuming that all disabled people want the same design.

Feeling Valued connects those practical outcomes to respect and intentional consideration. The organisation must still check whether the customer actually felt valued. A low satisfaction rating should initiate investigation; a high one must never cancel an unmet accessibility requirement.

Use the [outcomes guide](Satisfactory_Customer_Experience_Outcomes.md) to define tasks and measures, and the [research guide](Brand_Values_and_Inclusive_Experience_Research.md) to investigate the brand relationship.

## A brand is a promise that needs evidence

Brand values become useful when translated into behaviours and decisions. A value such as “putting people in control” could require clear choices, reversible actions, retained context and usable cancellation. The research question is whether people can exercise that control and recognise it, including when something goes wrong.

An organisation cannot compensate for exclusion by changing the language of its promise. When the promise and experience diverge, investigate the service, product, policy and handovers as well as the wording. Keep evidence that challenges the intended brand interpretation.

This version has documentation and export integrity checks. It has no participant study, deployed policy engine, jurisdiction-specific legal determination or validated brand outcome. Adoption requires named owners, an agreed scope and accessible research with people affected.
